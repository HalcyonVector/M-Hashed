Cascade Field Guide

A plain explanation of every marker, line, ring, and number in the coastal resilience simulator, plus an honest accounting of what's built against the original pitch document.

Udupi–Manipal–Kaup corridor · 32 assets, 25 with verified OpenStreetMap positions · last updated for the Round 1 prototype

01Nodes

Every dot, pin, and square on the map is one asset in the network. Click any of them to see its full name, elevation, and current status.

Points of interest — shown as pins

HospitalThe only pin type with a permanent label. 3 in the network: District Hospital and TMA Pai Hospital (Udupi), KMC Hospital (Manipal).
School3 schools. Click for name and estimated enrollment.
Flood relief centre2 real schools double-tasked as designated relief shelters — a common, real Indian disaster-response practice, not an invented facility.

Infrastructure — shown as small squares

SubstationPower source. 4 in the network. Click to see its rated capacity and current load.
Water pump / worksWater source. 4 in the network. Dashed outline shown here means "estimated" — see below.

Localities — shown as plain circles

Residential locality7 named localities (Malpe, Kodavoor, Kaup, Katapady, Manipal, Indrali, Brahmagiri), each with an estimated population and an equity weight.

Structural points — small, unlabeled

Bridge / crossing3 in the network. These are the network's real single points of failure: Malpe Bridge sits at a real, measured 0.0m elevation; the creek crossing near Kaup at 4.0m.
Road junction5 purely structural points that hold the road graph together for routing. Not real places, so they carry no label — a real map doesn't mark every intersection either.
Highway / grid anchorOne point representing the wider NH66 highway network beyond the modeled area — the "outside world" a road route can escape to.

02Status colors & rings

Every marker's fill color is its live status at the current water level. Rings around a marker add a second, independent signal.

Green — operatingHas power and water where relevant, and a surviving road route to a hospital.
Amber — cascade failure or cut offFailed indirectly (lost its sole power, water, or road connection), or still running but with no surviving road route to any hospital.
Red — direct flood failureIts own real elevation is at or below the current water level.
Teal ring — rescued by a backup tieIts normal source failed, but a substation or pump tied to it by an emergency backup line absorbed the load. Only reachable today via the Malpe pump → Udupi Town Water Works tie at the Extreme flood preset.
Pulsing amber ring — overloadedA substation or pump is now carrying more load than its rated capacity, usually from absorbing a failed neighbor's customers. This is a leading-indicator warning, separate from the impact score.
Solid outlinePosition and elevation confirmed against OpenStreetMap + NASA/USGS SRTM data.
Dashed outlineEstimated placeholder — OpenStreetMap had no tag for this utility in this area (true for every water pump, and one of the four substations).

03Dependency links

Power, water, road, and backup connections are a logical dependency graph, not physical routes. They're shown by default; the Hide dependency links button above the map switches to a decluttered, pins-only view if you want to read the coastline and status colors without the graph on top.

PowerSubstation → consumer. A consumer fails indirectly if every one of its power edges is cut.
WaterPump → consumer. Same rule as power, for water.
RoadThe routable network hospitals and shelters need a surviving path through, back to the highway anchor.
Backup tieEmergency cross-feed between two substations or two pumps. Only 4 exist, and Kaup's substation and pump have none — a real finding, not an oversight.
A failed edge turns red and dashed, the same convention as a failed node.

04Side panels

Critical

Betweenness centrality computed once on the intact network — the 6 assets whose removal would fracture the most connections, regardless of today's water level. This answers the brief's call to "identify disproportionately important assets" independent of any one scenario.

Routes

Pick two points; the app computes the shortest road route on the intact network (dashed grey) and the safest surviving route at today's water level (solid green), and tells you whether the shortest route is actually blocked and how much detour the safe one costs.

Interventions → This scenario

Five real, costed mitigations (raise a causeway, add a backup feeder, build a bypass) re-tested at today's water level, ranked by how much they cut the impact score.

Interventions → Robust plan

The same five mitigations evaluated as expected impact reduction across all four scenarios at once, weighted by illustrative likelihood, then solved as a knapsack against a budget you set. This is the direct answer to the brief's "compare alternative scenarios" ask — it optimizes across all of them, not just the one on the slider.

05Headline metrics

Cut off from every hospital
People in a locality with no surviving road path to any hospital, regardless of whether their own building has flooded. This is usually the largest and first-moving number.
Assets failed
Count of nodes in direct or cascade failure — a raw tally, not weighted.
Utility impact score
The brief's own formula.Σ (population served × equity weight)summed over every failed asset with a population.
Sources overloaded
Count of substations/pumps currently serving beyond their rated capacity via a backup tie — a leading indicator, kept separate from the impact score on purpose.

The two "Partial" rows, honestly explained

Capacity was specified on every node and edge in the original data model. It's implemented on substations and pumps (rated capacity, with the overload mechanic above), which is where it does real analytical work. It's not implemented on roads or bridges — a road-congestion capacity model is a real, larger feature genuinely deferred, not quietly dropped.
Edge-level flood threshold was meant to let a connection flood independently of either endpoint's elevation (a low-lying cable run between two higher points). Real elevation data made this almost unnecessary: Malpe Bridge and the Kaup creek crossing are themselves real, separately-elevated nodes at 0m and 4m, so the same effect happens through node elevation rather than a synthetic edge number with no data behind it. Judgment call: more honest than inventing edge thresholds, at the cost of matching the original schema exactly.
